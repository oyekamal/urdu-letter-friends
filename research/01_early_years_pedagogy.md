# 01 — Early-Years Pedagogy for Urdu Letter Characters (ages 3–6)

**Scope.** What is known about how 3–6-year-olds learn letters, what is specific to Urdu's Arabic-derived script, what game design helps or harms pre-schoolers, and what that means for animated letter-characters (Ba = duck, Alif = worm, Tay = leaf-with-butterflies).

**Builds on, does not repeat:** the older-learner research in `urdu-reading-course/research/` — `04_pedagogy.md` (phonics, letter order, positional forms, dots, Naskh vs Nastaliq, tracing), `10_learning_design.md` (session length, streaks, feedback), `11_letter_lesson_flow.md` (per-letter screen flow), `12_child_ux.md` (touch targets, attention spans, error handling) — and the decisions in `urdu-reading-course/course/00_design.md`. Where this file says "see 04 §3", read that file. Anything not confirmed from a source I actually opened is marked **unverified**.

## 1. How 3–6-year-olds learn letters

**Alphabet knowledge predicts reading — but teaching letters alone does not make readers.** Alphabet knowledge is "one of the best predictors of later word reading" (NELP 2008, NRP 2000, cited in Demchak & Solari, *Brick by Brick*, The Reading League 2025, https://www.thereadingleague.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Brick-by-Brick-Insights-on-Alphabet-Instruction-From-Research.pdf). The Piasta & Wagner (2010) meta-analysis (63 studies, 8,468 children) found instruction moves letter-sound knowledge (d ≈ 0.65) and letter writing (0.59) more than letter names (0.43), and found "minimal evidence of transfer" to reading or spelling at 2–12-month follow-up (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910925/). Design implication: letter characters are the *entry*, not the product; the app must blend letters into words early (see 04 §1 and 00_design row 1) or it is a toy.

**Names or sounds first?** For English, teaching names *and* sounds together beats sounds alone (Piasta et al. 2010; Roberts et al. 2018–2020, 83 preschoolers, four conditions, 10-minute sessions four days a week for nine weeks — combined name+sound lessons produced greater growth and engagement was high in all explicit-lesson groups; summarised in *Brick by Brick* pp. 22–23). Names help because most English letter names contain the sound (Treiman; Shanahan, https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/letter-names-or-sounds-first-you-might-be-surprised-by-the-answer). **Urdu is even more acrophonic**: بے پے تے ٹے ثے جیم چے خے دال… almost every name starts with its sound; exceptions are ا (vowel seat), ع, ء, and the semi-vowels و ی. So the English finding transfers: say the *sound* first, then the *name*, then the word ("b… bay… batakh"). The Pakistani classroom default is the opposite — names only, then "joṛ" spelling by names ("alif madd aa, meem sākin" for آم), which practitioners blame for children who can name letters but cannot read (Zulfiqar Ali, field observation, no outcome data, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/why-do-children-pakistans-public-sector-schools-learn-zulfiqar-ali-lcwjf). ITA's review of the SNC ECCE says the same in curriculum terms: letters-by-flashcard has "taken precedence over the oral blending and segmenting skills" and phonemic awareness is missing from the document (https://itacec.org/ece/document/2022/ITA_comments_ECCE_in_Single_National_Curriculum.pdf, Language & Literacy annex, points 3–4).

**Embedded (integrated) picture mnemonics — the direct evidence for our whole concept.** Ehri, Deffner & Wilce (1984, *J. Educational Psychology* 76) taught letters drawn *as* objects (s as a snake, h as a house) and found better letter-sound learning than pictures placed *beside* letters or no pictures (Shanahan, https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/a-question-i-hate-should-we-use-pictures-embedded-mnemonics-when-teaching-phonics). Shmidman & Ehri (2010, *Scientific Studies of Reading* 14(2):159–182) replicated it with 36 English-speaking pre-schoolers (mean 5;2) learning **Hebrew** letters — a foreign, non-Latin script — and found embedded mnemonics "enhanced the retention of letter–sound connections", needed fewer repetitions and reduced confusion (ERIC abstract https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ879303; quoted in PMC13043441, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13043441/, an 18-week Turkish first-grade trial, n=85, large effects on recognition accuracy ηp²=0.22 and speed 0.36). Roberts & Sadler (2018, *ECRQ* 46, 38 pre-schoolers, RCT, 32 × 25-min lessons): letter-sound ID d=1.31, initial-phoneme ID d=0.61, blending d=0.62 for Letterland characters vs plain letters, with equal motivation in both groups (Letterland-commissioned summary of a peer-reviewed study, https://files.letterland.com/pdf/Website-Downloads/USA/Letterland-Study-Validation-Summaries.pdf). The mechanism (dual coding + lower extraneous load because letter, sound and image are one unit) is spelled out in PMC13043441. Two conditions make it work, and both are testable on our assets: **the picture must be the letter's shape**, and **the picture's name must start with the letter's sound**. Shanahan's caveat: mnemonics help letter-sounds but *hurt* whole-word learning — pictures distract from the letters in a word. So characters belong on letter screens, not on word-reading screens. Zoo-phonics adds a body signal per animal; its evidence is vendor-reported (Gallagher 2003 via https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu) — **unverified**.

**Multisensory.** Handwriting beats typing for pre-school letter recognition (Longcamp et al. 2005; see 04 §8). Bara, Gentaz & Colé: visuo-haptic exploration of letters raised kindergarteners' letter knowledge and initial-phoneme awareness before decoding gains (abstract, https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1348/026151007X186643). Shanahan: "always incorporate writing". Song and gesture: Sesame's own advice is letter-of-the-day, form letters with the body, spot the letter in signs (https://sesameworkshop.org/resources/recognizing-letters/) — practitioner guidance, not a trial. The alphabet song teaches the *sequence of names*, not shapes or sounds (Reading Rockets, https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/phonics-and-decoding/articles/alphabetic-principle): a tune is fine, a 37-letter recitation is not the lesson.

**Pace and dose.** *Brick by Brick* is blunt: "introducing one letter per week is too slow" — "one letter per day (Sunde et al., 2019) or 3–4 letters per week (Piasta & Wagner, 2010)", with cycles of review and extra time on the hard letters. Jones, Clark & Reutzel's Enhanced Alphabet Knowledge model: a letter a day, brief lessons, several passes through the alphabet (https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/teal_facpub/404/). Sunde, Furnes & Lundetræ (Norwegian first grade, natural experiment) found faster letter introduction helped letter knowledge, word reading and spelling — **abstract only** (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245690). Which letters are easy: own-name letters, frequent letters, single-sound letters, and letters whose name contains the sound (Treiman; Kim et al. via Cambridge 2012, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/factors-associated-with-lowercase-alphabet-naming-in-kindergarteners/4E402E63AFF376A6A5E8343635624126). Sessions: 10 minutes was enough in Roberts et al.; Duolingo ABC's efficacy sample averaged ~1.5 h/week and dose predicted PALS-K gains (see 10 §3). Attention: 3–4-year-olds 6–12 min, 5–6 10–18 min (12 Part 1). **Rule: one new character per 3–5-minute lesson, one to three lessons a day, and every letter comes back at least three times over the following weeks.**

## 2. Urdu / Arabic-script specifics for this age

- **Isolated vs positional forms.** Qaida tradition teaches isolated forms first; Alif Baa shows all four at once; the Arabic child-spelling data (PMC7497809) says ligature/shape errors *increase* in grade 2 without explicit teaching (04 §3). For 3–6 the honest compromise is: the character *is* the isolated form; the joined forms appear only as a one-screen "watch me hold hands" morph when the child moves to words (00_design lesson step 3), never as a second set of shapes to memorise at age 3.
- **Dots are the letter.** ب ت ث ن ی پ ٹ share one body; only dot count and position differ, and these confusions are the largest sub-class of children's visual errors and do not fade with exposure (PMC7497809; arXiv 2210.12273; 04 §6). For a character design this is the single biggest risk: the body is the fun part and the dots are what make it a letter.
- **Which sound to teach.** The clipped consonant (/b/, not "ba"), then the name. Aspirates بھ پھ are digraphs, not letters, and teachers themselves mispronounce them (Zulfiqar Ali) — leave them out of the 3–6 set. Same-sound Arabic loan letters (ث ص س; ذ ز ض ظ; ت ط; ہ ح) are spelling, not new sounds, and are under 1.5% of running text (00_design row 9): teach one letter per sound first (ب ت ن م ک ل ا ی د ر س…), the twins later.
- **Letter order.** No trial exists for Urdu (04 §2). Frequency-first with confusable letters clustered *and contrasted* (00_design row 2–3) is the reasoned choice; the traditional alif-bay-pay order puts the five-way dot cluster in the first five letters, which is exactly what the error data warns against. Pakistani parents know the qaida order, so keep a "dictionary order" chart for them (00_design row 16).
- **Right-to-left.** No direct study (04 §6, unverified). Practical rule: every animation, trace and word-build moves right-to-left; never let a tile row or progress bar run left-to-right on a letter screen.
- **Naskh vs Nastaliq.** No legibility study for beginners; Nastaliq stacks, slants and relocates dots, which adds visual load; it is nevertheless the real target script (04 §5, 00_design row 6). For 3–6: draw characters on **Naskh-proportioned bodies** (Noto Naskh Arabic) so dot positions are stable, and show the Nastaliq glyph as a "grown-up" version on the letter's summary card only.
- **Pakistan's ECE curricula.** The SNC ECCE 2020 copies the Punjab 2017 framework almost verbatim; Sindh 2018 is the only one with separate 3–4 and 4–5 competencies. Language ELOs are generic — "recognize letters and familiar words in simple texts", "use pictures, symbols and familiar letters…", "listen to, identify and appreciate a variety of sound patterns, rhythms and rhymes" — with no letter list, no sequence, and (ITA's critique) no phonemic awareness stage and no order from oral sounds to letters (ITA 2020, pp. 9–10 and annex). Medium: mother tongue with gradual transition to Urdu; print materials specified in Urdu. Punjab/Sindh KG classroom practice: alphabetic method with names and joṛ (Zulfiqar Ali, observational). I could not open the Punjab 2017 or Sindh 2017 curriculum PDFs themselves (Scribd-hosted) — **unverified beyond ITA's comparison table**. Implication: nothing in the national curriculum prevents a sound-first, character-based approach, and the ITA annex explicitly recommends systematic synthetic phonics.

## 3. Game-based learning for pre-schoolers: what works, what harms

**The four pillars.** Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615569721): an app teaches when the child is **active** (thinking, not swiping), **engaged** (contingent response to every touch, free of distraction "such as pop ups"), when content is **meaningful** (tied to the child's world) and **socially interactive** (a responsive character or a real person), all in service of a stated learning goal. Their content analysis of top "educational" apps found most fail the pillars (Meyer et al. 2021, https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2021/03/How-educational-are-educational-apps-for-young-children-App-store-content-analysis-using-the-Four-Pillars-of-Learning-framework.pdf).

**What works (evidence in this repo's sources):** immediate corrective feedback on every response (NRP 2000, 10 §4); error-specific hints instead of buzzers (Duolingo whitepaper, 10 §4); bite-sized lessons independently completable (10 §3; Khan Kids' 3–5-minute units, 12 Part 1); unlocks tied to real milestones, not app-open streaks (04 §10); familiar characters — children learn more from characters they have a "parasocial relationship" with (Calvert, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-45102-2_9; the Sesame ETS evaluations found regular 3-year-old viewers beat non-viewing 5-year-olds on letter recognition and "the children who watched the show most learned the most", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesame_Street_research); formative testing with real children before release (Sesame's distractor method, same source; 12 Part 5 rule 10).

**What harms.** Radesky et al. (2022, *JAMA Network Open*, 133 apps used by 160 children aged 3–5): 80% contained manipulative design — navigation constraints 46%, lures (sparkles, rewards) 45%, parasocial pressure from characters 25%, fabricated time pressure 17%, ads 32%, roadblock ads >20 s 22%; free apps had three times the count of paid ones; only PBS KIDS apps had none "other than use of autoplay" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493). Common Sense: "children are particularly susceptible to autoplay" and to stars/coins/hatchable eggs that manufacture "artificial achievement" (https://www.commonsensemedia.org/kids-action/articles/kids-tv-has-rules-its-the-internets-turn). Streaks run on loss aversion and Duolingo itself drops them for its youngest users (10 §3). Fast-paced content and background TV impair executive function (Canadian Paediatric Society, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5823000/).

**Screen-time guidance.** WHO 2019: no sedentary screen time under 2; ≤1 hour a day at 2–4, "less is better"; reading and storytelling *with a caregiver* is the encouraged sedentary activity (https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more). AAP 2016: 2–5-year-olds ≤1 hour of high-quality programming, co-view, help the child apply it to the world (https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/138/5/e20162591/60503/Media-and-Young-Minds — abstract via search; page itself blocked). **Joint media engagement** (Takeuchi & Stevens 2011, Cooney Center): productive co-use needs mutual engagement, dialogic inquiry, co-creation, boundary crossing (carrying the letter into the day), intention to develop, and "focus on content, not control" — the interface must not make the adult say "don't touch" (https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/the-new-coviewing-designing-for-learning-through-joint-media-engagement/, PDF pp. 44–45). Counter-evidence: in the Duolingo ABC study, parent co-play did not add to literacy gains once child app-hours were controlled (10 §3) — so design for JME as a *quality* and *transfer* mechanism, not as the learning engine.

**Definitions for this project.**
*Healthy engagement*: the child returns because the next character is interesting, stops when the lesson ends, can name the sound away from the screen, and a parent can join without being told what not to press.
*Addictive*: the child returns to avoid losing something (streak, timer, pet), cannot find the exit, is nudged by a character to keep playing or buy, or is fed the next item without asking.

**Design rules that follow:** (1) no streaks, timers, leaderboards, coins or hatchable anything; (2) no autoplay — every next step is a tap; (3) the lesson ends itself with a calm close ("the duck is sleepy") and a visible way out on every screen; (4) characters never ask the child to continue or to buy; (5) no ads, no upsell inside the child surface; parental gate for settings (12 Part 1); (6) a daily cap that the app enforces gently (WHO/AAP: total screen budget is ~1 h, so the app should ask for 10–15 min of it); (7) a "do it together" prompt per letter for an adult — find the letter's word in the house — to make JME and transfer cheap.

## 5. What this means for our letter characters

1. **The picture is the letter, not next to it.** The character's silhouette must trace the Naskh glyph within a tight tolerance (test: overlay the resting pose on Noto Naskh Arabic; body stroke and glyph stroke coincide). This is the difference Ehri showed between embedded and adjacent pictures.
2. **Dots are sacred.** Each dot is rendered at the font's dot size, in the font's position, in the body's colour family, and stays visible in every frame. The Tay butterflies must land exactly where the two dots sit and stay still at rest; a butterfly that flutters off the letter has removed a phoneme.
3. **No fake dots.** Eyes, spots, buttons or bubbles that sit on or near the letter body will be read as dots (two eyes on a ب makes a ت). Faces go on parts that are not letter strokes, or use a clearly non-dot style (line eyes, not circles).
4. **The mnemonic word must start with the letter's sound.** بطخ (batakh) for ب passes. **A worm for ا fails** (کیڑا starts with /k/); use انار (anār) or a worm that is *called* something with alif. **A leaf for ت fails** (پتّا starts with /p/); the butterflies do — تتلی (titlī) — so make the butterflies the character and the leaf the perch. Check every character against this before drawing.
5. **Sound, then name, then word, on tap.** "b… bay… batakh." The clipped consonant first; the qaida name second so parents recognise it; one anchor word third. Native-speaker recordings, never TTS (12 Part 1; 00_design row 14 for the adult app is the exception, not the standard here).
6. **One tap, one thing.** Each tap produces exactly one contingent response (sound + one short animation ≤2 s). No idle autoplay loops, no second animation queued behind the first (four pillars "engaged"; 12 rule 8).
7. **The character earns its screen and then leaves.** Characters live on the letter screen and the trace screen. On word-building screens the plain glyph appears (Shanahan's whole-word caveat), with an optional "peek" to bring the character back.
8. **Trace the body, dots last, right-to-left.** Finger-tracing follows the character's stroke with a start dot, in the stroke order of the glyph, then the dots (11 §2 screen 5); accept only on-letter strokes (SimplyWrite via 12 Part 1).
9. **Pace: one new character per 3–5-minute lesson; up to three lessons a day; every letter revisited three or more times.** Introduce ب ت ن ی as a contrast set, not on four separate days (00_design unit 2).
10. **Feedback without fear.** Wrong tap: the character wobbles and the target sound replays; no red, no buzzer (10 §4; 12 rule 4). Right tap: a short delight animation from the character, no coins.
11. **Nothing that pulls the child back.** No streaks, timers, notifications, hatchable pets, ads or character-voiced nudges to continue or buy; the app closes the session itself and shows an exit on every screen (Radesky 2022; Common Sense; WHO/AAP one-hour budget).
12. **A parent card per letter.** One line in Urdu and Roman Urdu: the sound, the name, the anchor word, and one thing to find in the house — so the letter crosses the screen boundary (Cooney "boundary crossing"; Sesame letter-of-the-day).
13. **Test with 3–6-year-olds before shipping each batch of characters**, watching two things: do they say the *sound* unprompted a day later, and do they confuse dot-siblings less than with plain glyphs (Sesame's formative loop; Piasta's warning that letter knowledge alone does not transfer).
